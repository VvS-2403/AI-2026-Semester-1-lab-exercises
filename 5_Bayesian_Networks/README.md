## 6. Answers to Questions 1-14

**Question 1: Why is this decomposition useful for generating text?**
The chain rule decomposition transforms the complex problem of generating an entire sequence at once into a sequence of simpler, sequential predictions. At each step, the model only needs to predict the next single token based on the history, which aligns perfectly with left-to-right text generation.

**Question 2: What independence assumption is being made by this network?**
The first-order Markov model makes the assumption that the current word is conditionally independent of all words prior to the immediately preceding word, given the immediately preceding word: $P(X_t | X_1, ..., X_{t-1}) \approx P(X_t | X_{t-1})$.

**Question 6: How does the program choose the next word?**
The program can either sample from the probability distribution (probabilistic) or always choose the most probable word (greedy deterministic). Sampling introduces variability proportional to the distribution, allowing different sentences to be generated, whereas greedy selection always yields the single most likely path, leading to repetitive or deterministic output.

**Question 7: What happens if the program encounters a word for which no transition has been observed?**
The model will hit a dead end (a zero-probability context) and will crash or be unable to generate the next word unless smoothing techniques or a fallback mechanism are implemented.

**Question 8: If one of the totals is 0.87, what does this tell you about the implementation?**
It indicates a bug in the implementation of the conditional probability table. The probabilities of all possible next words given a specific context must sum to exactly 1.0. A sum of 0.87 suggests that either some counts are being lost, the normalization factor is incorrect, or smoothing is improperly applied.

**Question 9: Are the most probable predictions always the same as the words that you would personally expect?**
No, because the probability model is strictly constrained by its highly limited training corpus and short context window. Human linguistic expectations draw upon a vast amount of prior knowledge, semantics, and long-range dependencies that a simple n-gram model lacks.

**Question 10: Compare the two sets of generated sentences. Which mode produces more variation? Why?**
Sampling mode produces more variation. Greedy generation deterministically follows the single highest-probability path, repeatedly producing the same sentence for a given start word. Sampling picks the next word stochastically according to the learned distribution, exploring different plausible paths through the Markov chain.

**Question 11: How does the second-order model differ from the first-order model in terms of...**
1. **Graph structure**: Each node now has directed edges from the two preceding nodes ($X_{t-2} \rightarrow X_t \leftarrow X_{t-1}$) rather than just one.
2. **Conditional probability table**: The table is conditioned on pairs of words (bigrams) rather than single words, significantly increasing its dimensions.
3. **Amount of context available**: The model uses the previous two words for prediction, allowing it to capture slightly longer-range dependencies (e.g., distinguishing "the dog ran" from "the cat ran").
4. **Amount of data needed**: It requires exponentially more data to adequately estimate the probabilities, as the number of possible word pairs (contexts) is much larger than single words, leading to data sparsity.

**Question 12: Why does increasing the amount of context potentially improve prediction? Why can it simultaneously make the model harder to estimate from limited data?**
Increasing context provides more specific information to disambiguate the next word, improving prediction quality. However, the size of the conditional probability table grows exponentially with the context length. With limited data, most contexts will never be observed (zero counts), making the probabilities hard to estimate reliably without a massive corpus (the curse of dimensionality).

**Question 13: Why is Approach B preferable when constructing an intelligent system?**
Approach B (specifying the probabilistic model) forces the engineer to decouple the mathematical design from the code implementation. It ensures the builder understands what probability distribution is being modeled and how it's represented. This enables rigorous validation through probabilistic invariants (e.g., checking if probabilities sum to 1), whereas simply asking for "a language model" (Approach A) treats the LLM as a black box and yields code that is difficult to systematically verify or debug.
